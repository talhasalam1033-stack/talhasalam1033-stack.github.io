Course project, Structural Systems in Engineering Practices

Parametric study of a 200 m framed tube tower

A fully parametric Grasshopper and Karamba3D model of a 200 m steel framed tube tower, used to compare how uniform, corner-reinforced and height-graded perimeter columns trade lateral drift against steel use.

The question

The lateral system decides how a tall building resists wind, and it gets more expensive as buildings get more slender. We asked how three ways of placing steel in the perimeter columns of a framed tube change lateral drift, steel use and member utilization, and how many columns per face are worth having.

What we did

  • Built the whole tower parametrically in Grasshopper with Karamba3D: beam elements for columns and spandrel beams, meshed shell slabs for diaphragm action, fixed supports, S355 steel and C30/37 concrete.
  • Applied self weight, a 10 kN/m² imposed load and wind to Eurocode 1, and ran second order (P-Delta) analyses with automatic extraction of drift and the most utilized member.
  • Compared three layouts: uniform 1200 mm columns, 2000 mm corner columns, and columns stepped from 1200 mm down to 600 mm over four height zones.
  • Swept the number of columns along the wind direction from 5 to 13.

Results

LayoutTop driftSteel use vs uniformPeak utilization
Uniform tube9.8 mm100%17.90%
Thick corner columns8.1 mm107%15.21%
Height-graded columns10.9 mm85%17.95%

Thick corners give the least drift, because the corners add most to the global moment of inertia, but they carry the most steel. Grading the columns with height saves about 15% of the steel for an 11% increase in drift, since overturning moment and shear are largest at the base. The uniform tube shows clear shear lag, with the central columns of each face carrying less axial force than the corners.

What I learned

A parametric model turns design questions into quick experiments, and the shear lag concept explained results that the numbers alone did not. Every layout stayed far below its strength limit, so in this tower stiffness and steel use, not strength, drive the design.
